# Choice Kingdom — Story Bible

## 1. Creative promise

**Choice Kingdom** is a compact political-fantasy drama about ruling a fragile kingdom when every solution creates a new problem.

The player is not a superhero and not an omniscient strategist. They are a newly crowned ruler inheriting a country whose institutions, people, army, treasury, faith, merchants, and neighboring powers all want different things. The game is about deciding what kind of ruler the player becomes.

The central dramatic rule is:

> A good decision solves today's problem. A great decision also determines what tomorrow's problem will be.

Choices should rarely be simple good/evil selections. Each side should contain a legitimate reason, a hidden cost, and a person who benefits or suffers. The most interesting consequences should sometimes arrive several turns later, when the player has forgotten the original decision.

## 2. Setting

The kingdom is **Avelune**, a prosperous but politically brittle realm built around the River Vale. A generation of peace created wealth but also complacency. The old monarch died unexpectedly, leaving a young ruler with an incomplete succession plan.

Avelune has five pressure centers:

- **The Crown** — legitimacy, law, appointments, and the ruler's personal reputation.
- **The Commons** — farmers, craftsmen, dock workers, and townspeople who want food, safety, and predictable taxes.
- **The Houses** — old families who expect influence and resent reforms that weaken inherited privilege.
- **The Guilds** — merchants and financiers who can keep trade alive but expect favorable rules.
- **The Border** — soldiers and frontier towns facing a neighboring power that is not necessarily seeking war, but will exploit weakness.

The kingdom's problems are interconnected. A tax increase can fund soldiers but anger farmers. A trade concession can fill the treasury but empower a guild. A harsh punishment can deter crime but create martyrs. A generous pardon can heal a city while convincing rivals that the Crown is weak.

## 3. Tone

Political drama, human-scale fantasy, dry humor, occasional warmth, and consequences that can become tragic without becoming gratuitously grim.

Avoid:

- random cruelty for shock value;
- endless exposition;
- choices where one button is obviously correct;
- cartoon villains;
- characters who exist only to deliver quest instructions;
- fantasy lore that does not affect decisions.

Use:

- recurring characters who remember the ruler's behavior;
- callbacks to previous choices;
- conflicting loyalties;
- small human stories inside large political problems;
- moments of relief and humor between tense decisions;
- consequences that can be beneficial, harmful, or ambiguous.

## 4. Main characters

### Queen/King Elian Vale (the player)

The ruler's identity is deliberately player-defined. The game never forces a personality label. The history of choices becomes the personality.

### Chancellor Mara Vey

A disciplined administrator who believes institutions matter more than personalities. She is not heartless: she grew up during a famine and fears disorder more than she fears unpopular laws.

Relationship axis: **institutional trust**.

She rewards consistency. If repeatedly ignored, she may resign, secretly preserve evidence against the Crown, or become the architect of a constitutional settlement after a crisis.

### Captain Rowan Hale

Commander of the river guard. Brave, practical, and suspicious of court politics. He wants soldiers properly paid and civilians protected, but he can drift toward militarism if repeatedly empowered during crises.

Relationship axis: **security loyalty**.

### Lady Seris Auren

An old noble with a public reputation for elegance and a private reputation for ruthless bargaining. She initially appears to be an antagonist, but she genuinely believes the old houses are the only thing preventing mob rule.

Relationship axis: **aristocratic respect**.

She can become an ally, rival, reformist convert, or final political opponent depending on the player's treatment of privilege.

### Ivo Renn

A young guildmaster and merchant organizer. Charming, funny, and dangerously good at finding loopholes. He can make the kingdom rich or turn the economy into a private toll road.

Relationship axis: **commercial leverage**.

### Sister Amara

A healer and keeper of the House of Lanterns, a charitable institution trusted by ordinary people. She dislikes court politics but repeatedly becomes involved because policy decisions affect the poor first.

Relationship axis: **public conscience**.

### Toma Reed

A courier and occasional smuggler from the docks. He knows rumors before officials do. He can become a source of information, a liability, or an unexpectedly loyal friend.

Relationship axis: **street network**.

## 5. Core themes

1. **Legitimacy versus effectiveness** — a ruler can get results and still lose the kingdom's trust.
2. **Short-term relief versus long-term resilience** — emergency fixes create structural debts.
3. **Power changes people** — characters react differently when given authority.
4. **Information is a resource** — sometimes the correct decision depends on who the ruler believes.
5. **Mercy has a price, but so does punishment.**
6. **The ruler's legacy is constructed from patterns, not one heroic decision.**

## 6. Five-act campaign

### Act I — The Empty Throne

The player inherits a kingdom that looks stable from the palace and unstable everywhere else. The first choices establish relationships and reveal that the treasury is healthier than expected but the institutions are weaker than the previous ruler admitted.

Primary questions:
- Who gets access to the ruler?
- Does the Crown investigate before acting?
- Is the first priority money, food, security, or legitimacy?

### Act II — The Price of Peace

Trade grows, but the neighboring state of Veyra begins testing the border. Internal factions realize the new ruler can be shaped.

Primary questions:
- Does the Crown enrich merchants or protect local producers?
- Does it militarize the border or negotiate?
- Does it tolerate political opposition?

### Act III — The Hidden Ledger

The player discovers that the previous reign used secret emergency loans and informal favors. Someone has been profiting from instability.

The central mystery is not simply "who is corrupt?" It is whether the system itself makes corruption rational.

Primary questions:
- expose individuals or reform the mechanism;
- preserve stability or risk a scandal;
- trust officials, characters, or evidence.

### Act IV — The Winter of Three Fires

A harsh winter, a border incident, and a political scandal arrive together. Earlier choices now determine which resources, allies, and options are available.

The player can no longer repair everything. They must choose what the kingdom can afford to lose.

### Act V — The Crown's Answer

The kingdom reaches a final constitutional crisis. The ruler's previous pattern of behavior determines who stands with them, what institutions survive, and which endings are possible.

There is no single canonical "correct" ending.

## 7. Ending philosophy

At least six endings should be available, with several hidden combinations rather than one-stat thresholds.

- **The Steward** — institutions are stronger than when the ruler began; the Crown voluntarily limits its own emergency power.
- **The Iron Crown** — order survives through military authority; the kingdom is stable but freedom is sharply reduced.
- **The Golden Compact** — merchants and Crown build a prosperous commercial state, but economic dependence becomes the new vulnerability.
- **The People's Charter** — public trust and civic institutions become the basis of a new political settlement.
- **The Broken Diadem** — the kingdom fragments after too many unresolved pressures and betrayed relationships.
- **The Quiet Throne** — the ruler survives personally but withdraws from politics, leaving a fragile peace and an ambiguous legacy.
- **The Hidden Ending: The Second Founder** — requires discovering the ledger, protecting evidence, building cross-faction trust, and refusing several tempting emergency shortcuts. The player creates a new constitutional order without destroying the old one.

## 8. Consequence design rules

Every major event should be evaluated across four horizons:

- **Immediate:** what changes on the current turn.
- **Near-term:** what becomes possible or impossible within 1–4 events.
- **Delayed:** a consequence scheduled for a later turn or triggered by a condition.
- **Legacy:** how the decision changes a character, institution, ending, or future run strategy.

A choice can therefore be numerically positive while narratively dangerous.

Example:

> Granting Ivo a trade charter gives +18 gold immediately. Months later it can trigger price fixing if the player has repeatedly favored the guilds. If the player also invested in market oversight, the same charter can instead unlock a prosperity event.

## 9. Replay philosophy

A second playthrough should not merely show different text. It should expose different information.

Examples:

- A player who trusted Mara learns how to identify forged emergency orders.
- A player who trusted Toma learns rumors that reveal the same conspiracy through the docks.
- A player who empowered Rowan can resolve a border crisis militarily but may lose the peaceful diplomatic option.
- A player who empowered Seris may unlock noble mediation that is unavailable to a reformist ruler.

The same kingdom should therefore feel different depending on the relationships built during the previous run.

## 10. Content target

The first full commercial content target is **40–60 authored decision events**, including:

- 12 main-arc events;
- 12 character events;
- 10 systemic/economic/security events;
- 6 mystery/conspiracy events;
- 6 late-game crisis events;
- 6+ ending resolution events.

The event engine must support substantially more content without architectural changes.
